About

I came to machine learning by way of industrial engineering. My undergraduate work optimised physical systems — a CNC hot-wire foam cutter, automobile inbound logistics in FlexSim — where a model's value is measured by whether it survives contact with a real production line, not by how well it fits historical data. That standard followed me into data science.

I'm currently completing a Master of Science in Data Science at Adelaide University, where my research asks a narrower and, I think, more useful question than "which model performs best": which models can be trusted, under what conditions, and for how long. That means leakage-controlled evaluation protocols, statistical significance testing rather than single-run comparisons, and reporting calibration and temporal stability alongside accuracy.

I'm looking to continue this line of work at the PhD level — extending trustworthiness evaluation to foundation models for time-series forecasting, and to safety-critical domains like clinical signal classification, where a confidently wrong prediction is worse than a hedged one.

Research interests

Trustworthy foundation models

Evaluating time-series foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai) against classical and deep baselines for robustness, calibration, and temporal stability — not just point accuracy.

Time-series forecasting

Leakage-controlled, reproducible forecasting pipelines across noisy, non-stationary domains such as financial and energy data.

Healthcare AI

Patient-independent evaluation of clinical classifiers, with attention to minority-class performance where errors carry the highest cost.

Reproducible ML evaluation

Statistical rigor in model comparison — significance testing, uncertainty quantification, and evaluation design that resists silent data leakage.

Research & projects

Trustworthy Foundation Models for Time-Series Forecasting

2026

A leakage-controlled, reproducible forecasting framework comparing Chronos, TimesFM, classical models (ARIMA, SARIMA, Holt-Winters, exponential smoothing), LSTM, Prophet, and naive baselines across Bitcoin and electricity datasets. Models are evaluated on accuracy, statistical significance, robustness, temporal stability, and uncertainty calibration — treating trustworthiness as multidimensional rather than a single score.

MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Heartbeat Classification

2026

A patient-independent pipeline classifying four AAMI heartbeat classes from the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database (PhysioNet), comparing Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and CNN models. Evaluation uses per-class precision/recall/F1, precision-recall AUC, and McNemar's test to compare minority-class recall statistically, relating model complexity to minority-class performance.
